# Reading a Sector

Thinnest file so far — sector-level reasoning is less common in portfolio-journal threads
than stock-specific or methodology posts. Expected to grow fastest from the retroactive pass
over `hitesh-portfolio`, `phreaks-thoughts-ideas-and-opinions`, and `the-harsh-portfolio`,
where sector-rotation calls are more frequent.

## Separate the sector's real economics from the market's current narrative about it

Ghonarbochon (rank #6, elevated-tier) — *Investment journey of a late starter*, posts #191-192
(2026-02-12 / 2026-02-22), a two-part thesis on AI's impact on Indian IT services, written as
"only for reference later on what I thought... at this point in time" (i.e. dated deliberately
for future accountability). Core argument: IT services revenue is dominated by
transformation/upgrade/migration work, not the routine manual work AI targets first; the real
constraint on AI adoption inside large enterprises is deeply tangled legacy environments
("most do not even keep up with Microsoft OS versions and still have 2012 servers running"),
not model capability. Follow-up post traces a second-order effect worth the pattern-matching:
AI *hype* is making client companies demand more aggressive cost-reduction targets in
contracts regardless of whether AI can actually deliver them yet, which pressures IT services
margins indirectly (through unrealistic client asks) even before AI itself displaces any work.
**Pattern to reuse:** when a narrative claims a technology will disrupt a sector, check what the
sector's revenue actually comes from before accepting the disruption thesis at face value —
and look for indirect margin effects (client behavior changing) that can hit before the direct
effect (the technology itself) does.

## Reason about the sector's eventual equilibrium state, not the path or timing to get there

phreakv6 (rank #2, core-tier) — *Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #275
(2026-07-12): "Any system thats disturbed eventually returns to an equilibrium state. It need
not be the same state as it was before the disturbance. I like looking at what an equilibrium
state can be when analysing a sector. This makes me not sweat the immediate details." Two
worked examples from the same post: on IT services in January 2026, "I felt equilibrium for IT
stocks was a 12 p/e future, similar to Coal India. Most stocks were trading 20 p/e+ at that
time. I felt it might take time to get there though but its happening rather quickly now"; on
power transmission, he reasons from the fact that generation and consumption are separated
both geographically and temporally (so the grid must be strengthened to move power from where
it's generated to where it's used, and storage to move it through time) to arrive at "15000
ckm and 32 GW of HVDC transmission lines being added in the medium term... whether it happens
in 5 years or 7 years," without needing to predict the exact timeline. Explicit purpose stated
in the same post: "understanding equilibrium states for the stocks and sectors you own can be
a comforting thing that helps hold on to the conviction" through the noisy path of getting
there — a conviction-holding tool, distinct from the entry-timing rule in
`screening-methodology.md`.

## Separate directly-exposed from indirectly-exposed markets when a geopolitical shock hits, and expect a first-order panic followed by a second-order recovery

hitesh2710 (rank #1, elevated-tier) — *Hitesh portfolio*, post #8092 (2026-03-09), on reasoning
through a fresh war's market impact using the pattern from prior shocks (Russia-Ukraine,
India-Pakistan, earlier Iran-Israel): "First order effect of war is panic and pandemonium. This
can be followed by a rebound as ferocity of war reduces. Then second order effects start showing
up with countries most affected by wars taking long time to make a comeback." The specific
sorting rule he applies: a country not directly involved still corrects on initial panic, but
"will have lesser second order impacts" than a directly affected one — his example, India isn't
a war party but takes a "marginal impact due to crude impact and hence higher import bill" that
fades once "oil cools down and settles down in a range." Practical use stated in the same post:
don't try to call the bottom from the news itself — "observe stocks that are holding strong, or
better still... the first to start making 52 week highs, or break out of trading ranges" (see
`screening-methodology.md`'s resilience screen) as the signal that the second-order damage has
been priced in, then check whether fundamentals support acting on it. Follow-up post #8095
(2026-05-01), roughly two months into the same war outlasting his original expectation ("what
was expected to be a 2-3 weeks affair is not ending as expected"): the framework held up as a
process even though the specific war's duration was wrong — he continued tracking resilient
sectors (financials/microfinance, power and energy, metals) through the extended shock rather
than revising the panic/recovery model itself.

## Commit capital to a sector only after tailwind, then TAM, then moat, in that order — and pass on cyclicals/subsidy-dependent sectors absent a demonstrated ability to read the cycle

phreakv6 (rank #2, core-tier) — *Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #301
(2026-07-29): "Once you have decided a stock needs a commitment based on sector tailwind,
overall TAM for the business and company's capability (tailwind, tam and moat - in that
order), you must look at valuation." He states a specific sector exclusion list drawn from his
own track record, not theory: "all cyclical commodities like paper, sugar, financials,
textiles and sectors that survive on govt subsidy etc. are worth avoiding unless you have a
fairly good idea where we are in the cycle and how past cycles played out and how long you
have left - I never can figure these out with any reliability so I have learnt to pass on all
these." The exclusion is framed as a personal skill gap he's identified and worked around, not
a claim that cyclicals are universally un-investable — a useful model for writing one's own
sector pass-list honestly rather than as a borrowed rule.

## Tie a cyclical sector's revenue swings to one trackable external indicator, then use prior cycles as the base rate — while admitting when the current one won't fit them

harsh.beria93 (rank #3, elevated-tier) — *The harsh portfolio!*, post #547 (2024-02-24), on
calling the bottom of an agrochemical downcycle: "when FAO food price index goes down sharply,
farmers use less pesticides." He backs this with two prior cycles measured against Sharda
Cropchem and PI Industries: the 2009 downcycle (FAO index -22%) cut PI Industries' FY10 sales
growth to 5% (from 25% in FY09) and Sharda's FY10 sales -26%, both recovering "very quickly" as
food prices rebounded by FY11; the 2015 downcycle (FAO index -19%) cut PI's FY16 growth to 8%
(not recovering until FY19) and Sharda's FY16 growth to 15% (recovering by FY18) — the same
external trigger produced different recovery speeds in different cycles, which he records
rather than smooths over. Applied to the then-current downturn: "food prices peaked in May 2022
... down by 21% already but hasn't come down below 100, where in the past they have bottomed
out... Jan 2024 food prices are still 118 (very high by historical standards). So its hard to
call it a bottom." The discipline worth copying isn't the specific indicator, it's the method —
find the one external number the sector's revenue actually moves with, get a base rate from how
it played out last time, and say plainly when the current data doesn't yet match the pattern
rather than forcing a call.

## Treat "asset-light platform business with technocrat founders and quality PE backing" as a recognizable category, and value it on forward not trailing PE

Vivek_6954 (rank #4, elevated-tier) — *Vivek Gautam Portfolio*, post #1224 (2025-09-01):
"Track platform cos closely as these asset light cos founded by well educated first gen
technocrats backed by high quality PE players deserves special attention. Once op leverages
comes into play top n bottom-line can rise sharply. Key is to focus on fwd PE." Restated more
fully in post #1230 (2025-10-21), on the same group of names: "All these platform cos are asset
light high growth high op leverage stories under intelligent fanatics first gen IITian n likes
promoters backed by quality PE players who have already done the homework on quality of
promoters and balance sheet. Buy on dips these platform digital plays focussed on forward PE
few years down the line." The pattern has three stacked, checkable components rather than one
vague "growth stock" label: (1) asset-light business model, so revenue growth doesn't require
proportional capital, (2) founder background (first-gen technocrat/IIT-pedigree entrepreneurs)
and reputable PE-investor backing used as an outsourced diligence signal on promoter/balance-
sheet quality rather than doing that diligence from scratch, (3) the specific mechanism for why
current trailing valuation understates the opportunity — operating leverage hasn't kicked in
yet, so trailing PE looks expensive while forward PE, once margins expand, does not. Named
examples across both posts: Zomato/Eternal, Ixigo, BlackBuck. Caveat implicit in his own
position sizing on these names (see `position-sizing-and-risk.md`'s discussion of averaging up
only on confirming triggers) — the category is a reason to *watch* closely and buy dips, not a
substitute for checking that the operating-leverage story is actually showing up in results.

## Big-ticket durables resist e-commerce disruption for reasons that don't apply to groceries — don't extrapolate one category's online shift to another

ranvir (rank #10, elevated-tier) — *Ranvir's Portfolio*, post #1684 (2025-11-10), pushing back
on an assumption that offline consumer-durable retail (Aditya Vision, Vijay Sales, Croma,
Reliance Retail) is structurally threatened by online: "I have never bought a consumer durable
costing > 5k via online sources... if the delivered item has a fault/flaw/snag, just the thought
of returning it and waiting again for a fresh delivery is a nightmare... That won't happen with
a local [store] — u can get it replaced on the spot." Names the second reason separately: "there
r consumers like me who just can't buy without touch and feel (except in case of groceries/small
ticket items — eg all my groceries come from BlinkIt and literally nothing meaningful comes from
a nearby supermarket)." The useful structure isn't "offline retail is safe" as a blanket claim —
it's that the two frictions he names (return/replacement hassle, need for touch-and-feel) scale
with ticket size and product-fit uncertainty, which is exactly why groceries went online fast
while big-ticket durables haven't: a sector read should ask which of a category's specific
frictions quick-commerce/e-commerce actually removes, rather than treating "online disruption"
as one undifferentiated force.

## The market ignores customer/molecule-concentration risk for as long as the numbers keep coming — and re-prices it violently when they don't (phreakv6, rank #3, core-tier)

*Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #330 (2026-09-01, love=83), explaining why he
passed on Acutaas after being "bitten by Bluejet": "because of single molecule risk ... Turns
out market can turn a blind-eye when numbers are coming - same thing is happening in Navin
Fluorine and Anthem also and PI Inds in the past. Market simply doesn't care about the
concentration risk until things break. Or maybe market chooses to stay ignorant." The sector-
read takeaway for CDMO / specialty-chem names (and any business with one dominant customer or
one dominant molecule): a rich multiple that appears to "look through" concentration risk is
not the market having judged the risk acceptable — it is the market deferring the judgement
until a bad print forces it, at which point the de-rating is abrupt. Use it two ways: don't
take a peer's high multiple as evidence your concentrated name is safe, and size a concentrated
holding for the day the numbers stop, because that is when the risk gets priced all at once.
Caveat from his own record — he applied this filter to reject Acutaas and it kept running, so
the rule protects against the tail, not against opportunity cost in the interim.

## In auto-ancillaries, the biggest wealth creation has come from global-supply-chain integration, not from near-term sales/profit growth (OmkarT, rank #13, elevated-tier)

*Omkar's Portfolio Analysis and Discussion*, post #466 (2026-09-12): "Though most of market
participants like and focus on new product developments which are delivered to Indian OEs or
exported, the biggest wealth creator in auto ancillary space is Motherson which has created
wealth mainly building global supply chain. This is really a 'Terminal Value' I have in mind,
not too much focussing on near term sales/profit growth. Most of these companies have
manufacturing units integrated in global supply chains. They are not just export houses."
Screening implication for the sector: don't just underwrite an auto-ancillary name on its next
few quarters of order wins or margin trajectory — check whether it is becoming structurally
embedded (owned manufacturing, not just a vendor relationship) inside a global OEM's supply
chain, since that embeddedness is what compounded Motherson's terminal value over the long run,
not any single product cycle.
