# Investor Psychology

## Publicly auditing your own failed strategy is rare and worth weighting heavily

Mudit.Kushalvardhan (rank #16, elevated-tier) — *Mudit's Portfolio*, post #332 (2025-01-20),
in response to a question about his portfolio's performance: "if you are talking of very old
one with which I started this thread, then its performance is very bad. I have checked all
the stocks performance in last 2 years and 4 months, and mostly all stocks have underperformed
nifty in a big way." Most forum posters showcase winners; an unprompted, specific admission
that an entire multi-year strategy underperformed is a stronger trust signal about the
person's honesty than any individual stock call — worth remembering when weighting *this
user's* future claims, separate from whether the strategy itself is worth copying.

## Name the specific failure mode, not just "it didn't work"

Mudit.Kushalvardhan, post #367 (2026-07-10), reflecting on why rank-based momentum
underperformed for him specifically: "I entered quite late after the upmove has been in
advanced stage and also I exit only when, I have given up most of the gains… The better
strategy to enter early and to exit timely to protect most gains…is to watch the chart and
then enter…instead of blindly following the rank system." The diagnosis is mechanical
(late entry after the move is priced in, late exit after giving back gains) rather than vague
("bad luck" / "wrong stocks") — a useful pattern to look for when reading anyone's post-mortem:
specificity about *why* is a better signal than the post-mortem existing at all.

## Sell a profitable position on loss of qualitative conviction, not just on price

Ghonarbochon (rank #6, elevated-tier) — *Investment journey of a late starter*, post #103
(2024-05-10): held Intellect Design Arena 3.5 years, price round-tripped from a 40% loss back
to a 55% profit, and he exited anyway — "booked out with 55% profit And zero satisfaction...
going to be very nice not to worry about their endless products that solves all banking issues
but adds nothing to the bottom line." The exit trigger was the thesis feeling exhausted, not a
price target or a red flag — a different (and harder to systematize) signal than the
mechanical rules in `screening-methodology.md`.

## "Close the chapter on a high" — explicit preference for early exit over riding a decision down

Ghonarbochon, post #185 (2025-12-17): "Sold out of Apollo Microsystems and Stallion Fluoro as
I do not have enough conviction... it seems better to close chapters on a high than suffer
through a long consolidation or downward leg." States the preference explicitly as a policy
rather than a one-off decision — useful to note because it's the opposite instinct from "let
winners run" (see `position-sizing-and-risk.md`); the two aren't contradictory so much as
applied to different situations (a compounding winner vs. a name whose thesis has gone stale).

## Write the bear case for your own position at the moment you add to it

Ghonarbochon, post #179 (2025-11-14), titled by him as a "Selfnote to remind myself why I
added Ceinsys now(incase this decision goes south later)": lists the specific risks he sees
(rising receivables/cash-flow risk, dependence on winning new large projects, unresolved CEO
succession, an acquisition promised but not yet delivered) immediately alongside the reasons
for buying. A concrete, repeatable habit — write the counter-thesis at entry, dated, so a
later re-read can check whether the original risks materialized rather than relying on memory.

## Define the sell trigger at the moment of buying — and treat "someone will pay more" as the tell of a bad trade

phreakv6 (rank #2, core-tier) — *Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #275
(2026-07-12): "selling is not separate from buying. Its not something done in isolation. While
buying you should have a good idea of why you are buying and what will make you sell it" —
concrete list given: "a valuation bridging or a tailwind coming to an end or govt policies
changing adversely... or serious balance sheet issues or corp governance issues or repeated
promises on growth or margins not kept." He then names the specific failure mode he's checked
against his own trade history: "It most definitely cannot be 'someone will buy it from me
higher' - I have only lost money on average when my thesis is this... Build a portfolio time
machine and analyse if your decisions are generating alpha... Makes you very grounded and
honest, at least to yourself." A companion post the same week (#268, 2026-07-08) names the
same trap at the market level: "When the entire market becomes a trend-following market,
things become zero-sum very quickly. No one makes money on average... Whenever my objective is
this... there's a good chance the greater fool is me." What makes this more than a generic
"don't be greedy" warning is the recommended check: audit realized trades in aggregate for the
pattern, not single anecdotes.

## Choosing to stay invested through fear is a deliberate policy, honestly reported as a work-in-progress rather than a settled belief

hitesh2710 (rank #1, elevated-tier) — *Hitesh portfolio*, post #8115 (2026-07-30), reflecting on
staying fully invested through consecutive Russia-Ukraine and Iran war shocks: "I have never
been out of the market for more than a month or two since past 15 years or more. Earlier I used
to take cash calls based on my reading of overall market, and it often cut both ways. So in the
end I decided that the way to go was to stay invested in companies in which I had conviction."
He names the payoff explicitly: "A couple of stocks in a concentrated portfolio going up
multifold... can create wonders for the portfolio returns" — and the cost, in the same breath,
without minimizing it: "There has been a lull in portfolio performance from time to time where
portfolio value has gone down nearly 30-40% from peak value, but overall returns have been
satisfactory." What makes this worth recording over a generic "stay invested" aphorism is the
closing admission: "For someone who can be nimble, taking cash calls can be quite rewarding.
Its been long on my wishlist to go back to those kind of strategies, but its a work in
progress... I have been slow in changing my style that has worked for me for so long" — he
states outright that inertia, not conviction the current style is optimal, is part of why he
hasn't changed it.

## Switch to a longer chart timeframe to stop reacting to noise you've already decided to hold through

phreakv6 (rank #2, core-tier) — *Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #268
(2026-07-08): "One trick I use is to only use monthly charts - all the volatility and 20%
drawdowns look routine in those - the noise is tremendously reduced... In general, its better
to buy few businesses many times than buy/sell many businesses once." Framed as a fix for a
specific, named behavioral stage, citing the book *The Biggest Bluff*: "Once you have mastered
the art of not losing (knowing what to avoid), it is important to transition towards playing
to win" — i.e. the technique is for a name he's already vetted and decided to hold, used to
resist selling into a routine pullback, not a substitute for the vetting itself. He backs it
with his own multi-bagger history: names like Apar, Shilchar, VBL, Wockhardt, Ceinsys and
Shaily each had "5 or 6 pullbacks 20%+, few even had 30/40% pullbacks though they all were 4x
in ~1 yr" — "the old me would have taken a 20% profit in these."

## Naming your own portfolio's drawdown risk in advance, then owning it without spin when it lands

harsh.beria93 (rank #3, elevated-tier) — *The harsh portfolio!*, post #570 (2024-08-21), on why
he hadn't been able to build cash despite trying: "I have been selling a number of stocks which
have done well... and reinvested in some new bets... I am also worried that in the next
downturn, portfolio might see sharp drawdowns given how rewarding this upcycle has been. Lets
see what future holds." Eight months later, post #628 (2025-04-13), after the Jan-Mar 2025
correction actually hit his portfolio harder than Nifty: "this is the reason behind my earlier
comments that during the next downturn, my portfolio will fall more than Nifty. And this is
clearly visible in this downturn (not something I am proud of, but atleast I could foresee
it)." The value isn't that the prediction was right — it's that the risk was named, dated, and
specific *before* it happened, and the follow-up neither claims credit for calling it nor hides
the discomfort of being right about something painful (see the standard-deviation-vs-beta
reasoning behind the call in `position-sizing-and-risk.md`).

## When praised for an extreme return, volunteer the tailwinds that did the work rather than let the skill narrative stand uncorrected

Vivek_6954 (rank #4, elevated-tier) — *Vivek Gautam Portfolio*, post #1212 (2025-01-14) stated a
tracking figure of his portfolio compounding 80x since 2012. Another member did the math in post
#1213 and called it "record breaking" at an implied 44% CAGR over 12 years, asking whether it
was achieved "without any incremental capital addition." Vivek_6954's reply, post #1214
(2025-01-15): "Yes not much additional capital was deployed as EMIs ate a major chunk of salary.
But base was quite small in 2012. Also the valuations were cheap for a no of years from 2012
onwards. Also booming Indian stock market helped a lot in recent years specially in mid n small
caps several have been named in the thread." Three specific, checkable factors volunteered
unprompted, each one deflating the "pure stock-picking skill" reading of the headline number: a
small starting base inflates multiple-based returns mechanically, cheap entry valuations were a
multi-year environmental condition not a personal edge, and the mid/small-cap bull run supplied
returns broadly rather than only to his picks. The habit worth noting isn't the modesty as such
— it's that the correction was offered in direct response to public praise, when simply
accepting the "record breaking" framing would have cost him nothing.

## A correction has recognizable emotional phases, not just a price path

hitesh2710 (rank #1, elevated-tier) — *Hitesh portfolio*, post #7961 (2025-01-27), narrating the
fifth month of a drawdown: "Usually at beginning of a correction there is a lot of false bravado
because previously buy on dips strategy always worked and this time it has not. But a lot of
investors keep buying these dips. Next phase is the fearful phase, where doubts start creeping
in about the buy on dips strategy... Next phase comes the phase of panic and capitulation when
markets correct sharply with big swings, and often gaps down frequently... This kind of market
induces a sense of numbness for investors wherein they cannot act, either to buy or sell." The
sequence (false bravado → creeping doubt → panic/capitulation → numbness) is offered as a
recognizable pattern in real time, not a retrospective label — useful less as a timing tool and
more as a check on which phase's emotional distortion is currently coloring one's own
decisions.

## Whether to sit out a non-performer or exit it is a function of investor temperament, not a universal rule

hitesh2710 (rank #1, elevated-tier) — *Hitesh portfolio*, post #7823 (2024-08-20): "How to deal
with non performers in the portfolio will depend a lot on the investor mindset... There are some
investors who can sit out the period of non performance for long time and then stock price tends
to move in their favor. I have seen folks like Donald and Ayush do that successfully. For a
person like me, it does not suit my mindset and hence after a decent period of time if stock
does not seem to be performing as per expectations, I tend to exit, especially when there are
other clear cut opportunities... this depends a lot on how an investor is wired." Notable for
explicitly declining to prescribe a single correct answer — he names two other well-known
forum investors as the counter-example (successfully waiting out non-performers) rather than
framing his own exit-on-underperformance habit as the right approach, then states plainly that
which one is correct depends on the investor's own psychological makeup.

## Building conviction to a large position is hard, and drifting into diversification isn't always a strategy — sometimes it's just admitted fatigue

vikas_sinha (rank #6, elevated-tier) — *The Anti-Portfolio*, post #679 (2024-08-30), asked how
long he'd been investing after a member praised his ~38x return since 2020: "It's gotten to the
point of lesser interest now after this dream run. I don't care much about portfolio
concentration now since I am lazy. Conviction is hard to build up to large position." Most
portfolio-concentration commentary on the forum frames diversification as a deliberate risk
choice; this instead names the honest driver as reduced engagement after a big run, not a
considered sizing philosophy — worth noting as a caution when reading anyone's stated
"diversified by design" rationale, since the same portfolio shape can come from either.

## Broken management promises are a standalone exit trigger, independent of whether the business recovers

Ghonarbochon (rank #9, elevated-tier) — *Investment journey of a late starter*, post #199
(2026-08-26), explaining a recent exit from Ceinsys Tech: "Its cheap now but management has
broken too many words to get market faith back soon even if they do well otherwise. But have
my eyes on this one." He separates two things that are easy to conflate — business performance
(which he concedes may be fine, and the stock is cheap) and management credibility (repeatedly
missed commitments). A record of broken guidance is treated as its own reason to be out,
because the re-rating needs market trust and that takes time to rebuild regardless of results.
Note he keeps it on the watchlist rather than blacklisting it — the trigger is "out until
trust is re-earned," not "never again."

## The analyst's dispassionate number-read and the operator's lived reality of a bad year are two different pictures — hold both before passing judgment

joinjp2003 (rank #7, elevated-tier) — *My portfolio updates and investment journey*, post #278
(2026-08-29): reading an annual report, he caught himself compressing a whole year into one
verdict — "Topline grew 25% while earnings grew only 5-7% ... margins fell, flop show for a
richly valued company" — then set it against the same company's MD&CEO letter describing that
year from the inside: US tariff shock, China rare-earth-magnet ban, the chairman's sudden
death mid-year, a ~40% revenue collapse from the single largest driveline customer (a model
that "did not work as expected"), MSCI-index deletion forcing ~$180m of mechanical outflows
"sending our stock to levels that bore no relationship to the underlying business", then
Iran-war input inflation. The point isn't to excuse a weak print — it's that a purely
metric-driven read ("margins fell → flop") silently assumes the drivers were controllable and
recurring, and a year like this one is mostly neither. Checkable habit: before marking a bad
year against management, separate the P&L damage into (a) controllable/structural vs (b)
exogenous shocks stacked into one fiscal, and check whether the depressed multiple is pricing
the business or the forced-selling. (Company in the example: Sona BLW Precision Forgings.)

## Asymmetric regret as a calibration signal: near-zero FOMO on conscious rejects, real regret only on "liked it, didn't act" (phreakv6, rank #3, core-tier)

*Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #330 (2026-09-01, love=83): "I realise I have
almost no fomo when I reject something consciously or when I never made the effort to study
something ... I have serious regret though when I like something but I am too lazy to act
(Aegis for eg. I had done all the work in Feb and I loved how cheap it was but never bought
since I liked the DC plays better - but in May, I had a good opp. to switch but I didn't)."
And on ideas he worked hard on then rejected that later ran (Acutaas, HFCL, STL, E2E): "When
an idea I work a lot on only to reject does well, it still makes me feel good because it means
I am fishing in the right areas." The usable structure: treat your *own* regret as diagnostic.
A missed stock you consciously ranked below the line and feel nothing about → the process
worked. A missed stock that keeps stinging → you liked it, did the work, and failed to act;
that is an execution failure to fix, not a research failure. A hard-worked reject that runs →
neutral-to-positive, evidence you're hunting in the right pond. Only the middle case is a
problem worth changing behaviour over.

## Pre-commit to the sectors you will never look at, so attention isn't taxed by things you'd sell in a fortnight anyway (phreakv6, rank #3, core-tier)

*Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #330 (2026-09-01): "There are whole swathes of
market I simply don't bother to look at - Sugar, Cement, FMCG, Textiles, BESS, Solar, Media,
Alcohol, Real Estate, Banks/NBFCs, Logistics, Bulk chemicals etc. Others can make money in it
but I know I won't. I will invariably sell out a week or two after buying because they don't
excite me (happened with Banks/NBFCs twice in the past for me). Avoiding such large swathes
makes it easy for me to focus on the things that matter." The circle-of-competence idea
restated as a *negative* list you write down in advance: name the sectors you have twice-proven
you can't hold through a wobble, and refuse to spend research time there regardless of how the
screen scores them. He frames the boundary as dynamic — "Try to stay within it (CDMO/Data
centers/Power for now) but keep expanding it (Oil & Gas)" — i.e. the exclusion list is
deliberately revised, not permanent, but expansion is a conscious project (he built the O&G
understanding over months) rather than opportunistic drift into whatever is running.
