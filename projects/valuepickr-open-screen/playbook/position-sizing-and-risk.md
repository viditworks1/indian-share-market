# Position Sizing & Risk

## Bucket portfolios by risk tolerance, not by conviction alone

Ghonarbochon (rank #6, elevated-tier) — *Investment journey of a late starter*, post #167
(2025-08-29): explicitly splits the portfolio into three buckets with different rules for
each, rather than a single conviction-ranked list —
1. **Core (≤20% of portfolio):** very small/micro caps, "depend on the chance that 1 in 5 will
   turn a true multi-bagger... take away my capital and some profits when it goes more than
   double or triple, and keep the rest for long term."
2. **Longterm (~40%):** small/mid/large cap, no predetermined exit price — "mostly business
   performance that decides my length of investment."
3. **Trades (remainder):** short/medium swing and momentum, "usually on some relatively well
   known names" for safety.

Reasoning given (same post): "I am not cut out for concentrated investing or Deep
value-investing because of my lack of patience" — the bucket structure is presented as a
fix for a self-diagnosed behavioral weakness, not a universal framework.

## Equal-weight + let winners run, don't pre-decide position caps

Mudit.Kushalvardhan (rank #16, elevated-tier) — *Mudit's Portfolio*, post #274 (2024-09-22):
"There are no tactics. All are equal weighted. Since 20 stocks in total, each one is 5% to
start with. After that, I let the winners grow to whatever size they go, till they are in
ranking list." Position size becomes an *output* of performance (winners compound their
weight) rather than an input decided upfront — but this only works paired with his automatic
exit rule (see `screening-methodology.md`) that removes decayed names from the ranking list,
otherwise winner-runs-free would concentrate risk unmanaged.

## Diversification as capital preservation, not just return-smoothing

Ghonarbochon, *Investment journey of a late starter*, post #100 (2023-12-30): "It does not pay
to be overcautious in Bull Market while picking stocks if one is not into very concentrated
investing... one is not supposed to bet too heavily in a single stock unless extremely
experienced." Framed less as "diversification reduces volatility" and more as a guardrail for
investors without the experience to size conviction correctly.

## Going to cash under macro uncertainty — honestly reported as inconclusive

Ghonarbochon, post #151 (2025-04-09): went 25% cash on Trump tariff/recession fears, sold
several names pre-emptively, then the market reversed sharply the next day. His own
conclusion: "Lesson learnt: Nothing. It was too turbulent and scary to stay all in... I should
probably do the same again in future if this kind of uncertainty appears." Worth recording
precisely *because* he doesn't claim the timing worked — a rare honestly-inconclusive risk
management post rather than a survivorship-biased "I called it" narrative.

## Automatic, criteria-based exit rules remove discretion from the hardest decision

Mudit.Kushalvardhan, post #287/#289: exits triggered mechanically — "If the stock price falls
more than 20% from its 52W high" or "the stock falls below the worst rank during rebalance."
The rule is defined by the *same* screening criteria used for entry (see
`screening-methodology.md`), so a stock's exit isn't a separate judgment call, it's just the
entry criteria failing in reverse.

## Position size should be gated by earned conviction, not a preset target — and scaled up in tranches as that conviction confirms

phreakv6 (rank #2, core-tier) — *Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #301
(2026-07-29), responding to a poster who was bullish on a name but sized it at 1%: "I think
you should improve the quality of your conviction. If you are bullish... and feel it in your
gut, you would not have stopped at 1% allocation... the best ones that have made money for me
are the ones where I couldn't get sleep because I did not have enough in the position - even
at 15%. It should feel slightly insane. But I wouldn't recommend blindly scaling it and
feeling nervous... You should feel a good nervousness and not anxiety." His stated mechanics
for sizing once that conviction exists (same post): start at 10%, be "ready to scale it up to
20% when valuation comes in favour," using 20-30% pullbacks as a reason to add rather than
sell — "this is my preferred method (pyramiding up) and I have done it in almost every
position of mine." Caveat, stated in post #275 (2026-07-12) on the same theme: "Stupidly
scaling up without having the skill is a recipe for disaster" — he frames concentration as a
skill built over years through sector/company depth work (see `reading-a-sector.md`), not a
starting posture.

## After exiting a big winner, rebuild breadth with small starter positions before re-concentrating

hitesh2710 (rank #1, elevated-tier) — *Hitesh portfolio*, post #8064 (2025-11-17), responding to
a question about what to do after exiting a high-allocation winner: "Once you exit a big winner
with very high allocation, the next step is slightly arduous... If I don't find something that
deserves high allocations at the beginning, what I have found useful is to have a few stocks
(max 5 stocks) starting position and take things from there." His stated sourcing method for
that shortlist is the sectors currently showing relative strength (see
`screening-methodology.md`'s resilience screen), then "monitor how the fundamental and technical
momentum starts developing in these... atleast a couple of these stocks will announce themselves
with the requisite characteristics, and then it becomes easy to scale up positions." He's
explicit this doesn't happen on a reliable schedule: "Getting stocks worthy of high allocations
right from the beginning does not happen too regularly for me... Maybe once every year or two."
Breadth-first, concentration-earned — the mirror image of phreakv6's tranche-scaling rule above,
applied to *which* stock gets the tranches rather than how big each tranche is.

## Only self-researched conviction survives the volatility that a large allocation produces

hitesh2710 (rank #1, elevated-tier) — *Hitesh portfolio*, post #8056 (2025-10-31): "Conviction
based on own research is very important here, because often stock prices correct 10-15-20% post
your buying, and borrowed conviction does not help in these situations." He gives his own
holding (HBL Engineering) as the concrete case for why this matters specifically at high
allocation sizes: it "hit levels of 700 plus in August 2024, then went down to 515 in Oct and
Nov 2024, and then again hit 700 plus in Dec 2024, and again declined to levels of 405 in March
2025. Digesting these kind of moves with allocations exceeding 20-25% of portfolio is a tough
ask, and needs a huge amount of conviction and temperament." Framed as a skill built through
repetition, not a starting posture: "Once you have ridden a big winner with huge allocation,
that experience helps in future in similar situations" — the same "concentration is a skill"
theme phreakv6 states independently above, from a different angle (surviving the round-trips
rather than deciding to take them on).

## Treat overall concentration level as a dial that tracks market conditions, not a fixed number of names

phreakv6 (rank #2, core-tier) — *Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #312
(2026-08-11): "Concentration is a function of market environment. When I feel that broader
market is getting weaker, being concentrated on names that give you valuation/growth comfort
protects the downside." He gives his own dated history as the evidence, not a general claim:
pre-2023 he ran ~10 stocks with the top 5 at 60-70%; by Sept-Dec 2025, bearish on the broader
market, he had reduced to just 3 positions (Sai Life, Hindustan Zinc, Silver/Gold); by June
2026, sensing the market "turning broader," he had spread into roughly eight small (~5% each)
positions adding up to 40-45% of the portfolio while trimming his largest holdings. He also
states a separate, practical tracking-capacity ceiling in the same post, independent of
conviction: "~10-15 stocks is the most you can closely track (tracking is not looking at price
and social media chatter) with an hour a day and several hours on weekends... or give your
money to a capable fund manager." Two distinct constraints — conviction/market-driven
concentration, and a hard attention-bandwidth ceiling — worth checking separately, not
collapsed into one rule.

## Standard deviation, not beta, is the risk metric that actually held up

harsh.beria93 (rank #3, elevated-tier) — *The harsh portfolio!*, post #628 (2025-04-13):
tracking his own portfolio's beta on a rolling 1-year monthly basis, he found it had
"consistently stayed below 1" despite "80%+ allocation to small caps" — and flags this as a
false comfort rather than a badge of skill: "Portfolio volatility has been quite high, and
beta looks low because of increasing divergence of my returns with Nifty returns as a result
of more bottom-up stock picking... next time a fund manager boasts their alpha and beta, ask
them about the standard deviation of their portfolio." His standard deviation, unlike beta,
had been "consistently higher than Nifty since July 2023" and had already flagged the risk
that then materialized in the correction he was reporting from — monthly returns of
-7.99%/-8.49%/+9.06% (Jan-Mar 2025) against Nifty's -0.58%/-5.89%/+6.30% over the same months.
Practical takeaway: for a bottom-up, low-correlation-to-index portfolio, beta understates risk
because it measures co-movement with the index, not dispersion of outcomes — standard
deviation is the metric to check before assuming a historically low beta means low risk.

## Position size as the tool for a governance risk you can't fix but still want exposure to

harsh.beria93 (rank #3, elevated-tier) — *The harsh portfolio!*, post #542 (2024-02-10), asked
about Garware Hi-Tech paying roughly 30-35% of PAT as a fee to an unlisted related party: "Yes,
it is an issue. But I cannot control it except with position sizing and trying not to overpay.
While accepting governance issues, I still want to participate in their growth opportunity.
Its a very very good business." Distinct from screening the stock out entirely on the
governance flag, or holding it at full size and hoping — the known, uncontrollable risk is
priced into *how much* he owns and *what he pays*, not into whether he owns it at all.

## Average up only on concrete, recurring confirming data — not on price strength alone — then let inactivity do the rest

Vivek_6954 (rank #4, elevated-tier) — *Vivek Gautam Portfolio*, post #1223 (2025-08-16): "Force
motor gave excellent returns in last few months to become my top holding as I average on upside
as well based on triggers like good monthly sales, new orders from defence n state govt for
ambulances." The named triggers are operating data (monthly sales prints, specific new order
wins), not the stock's own price action — a different basis for pyramiding than phreakv6's
"scale up as valuation comes into favour" rule (see above): here the confirmation is business
performance repeating itself, checkable independent of the chart. He repeats the same pattern
on two other names the same year — post #1227 (2025-09-30), Banco Products: "Averaged on upside
post good nos"; post #1231 (2025-10-22), Acutaas Chemicals: "averaged on upside post good nos
in last 2 qtrs" — in both cases tied explicitly to quarterly results, not a price trigger. Once
a position is built this way, his stated default is to leave it alone: same post #1223, on a
cluster of older large holdings (HBL Engg, Nuvama Wealth, MCX, Aurionpro, VBL, Uno Minda) held
"peacefully since last few years": "Inactivity pays if execution is good by ethical mgmt and opp
size is large." The two halves work together — the trigger discipline decides when to add, and
the inactivity discipline is what stops him from re-trading a position once the triggers stop
producing new information.

## What counts as a "big bet" scales with how concentrated the rest of the portfolio already is

hitesh2710 (rank #1, elevated-tier) — *Hitesh portfolio*, post #7847 (2024-08-29), titled "BET
BIG ON YOUR BEST IDEA": "For a reasonably diversified investor say having 15-20 stocks, levels
of 10-15% allocation may be big whereas for those who hold less than 10 stocks, anything between
20-30% is big. I have seen folks take nearly 40-50% allocation on their best ideas. It is for an
investor himself to decide what is big for him/her." The framing is deliberately relative, not a
fixed percentage rule — "big" is defined against the investor's own number of holdings, so the
same 15% allocation can be a minor position in a 20-stock book or an outsized bet in a 6-stock
one. He ties the underlying claim ("the difference between the men and boys is in the kind of
outsized bets the successful investors manage to take") to conviction earned through repetition,
consistent with the "concentration is a skill" theme recorded elsewhere in this file.

## When forced to raise cash, the exit order should follow conviction rank, not what's easiest to sell

hitesh2710 (rank #1, elevated-tier) — *Hitesh portfolio*, post #7720 (2024-04-29), responding to
a question on getting into cash: "While trying to get into cash the preferred exits would be in
lowest conviction or lowest performing stocks. The short term momentum trades would obviously be
the first ones to go under the hammer. Exiting longer term bets would be in case of extremely
extended unsustainable moves. (this is easier said than done.)" He states a clear three-tier
priority — weakest-conviction/underperforming names first, momentum trades next, core long-term
bets last and only on clearly overextended moves — while explicitly flagging that following this
order in practice is harder than stating it as a rule.

## Concentration is a precondition (depth of knowledge beating the market), not a default; a breadth-first style is a legitimate alternative

ranvir (rank #5, elevated-tier) — *Ranvir's Portfolio*, post #1627 (2025-08-30), responding to a
comparison against more concentrated investors who "have been generating much better returns...
over a long period of time": "one should concentrate when one's depth of knowledge about a
company is meaningfully better than the mkt. That comes with a lot of hard work. One should also
be ready to change his mind quickly, if the original thesis doesn't play out. I generally enjoy
reading about different businesses and try and build on the breadth of my knowledge (vs the
concentrated guys who work on depth). These are two different styles." He pairs this with a
concrete, checkable re-allocation rule he actually runs: "Re-allocation is broadly based on...
the earnings momentum that a business is displaying. Ones performing well get added on dips.
Others are trimmed on rallies" — sizing driven by realized operating performance rather than a
fixed target-weight rebalance.

## "Muft ka Laddu" — pull your cost basis out of a doubled winner and let the house-money remnant ride

Ghonarbochon (rank #9, elevated-tier) — *Investment journey of a late starter*, post #199
(2026-08-26): as several holdings doubled (Shilpa, SBCL, Syrma) he "taken out capital from
them or sold altogether"; Naveen Fluorine and Dynamatic were "sold with good profits in excess
of 40%" but he keeps "some Dynamatic bought entirely of the profits made in it." A whole ~20%
slice of the portfolio (HBL, Apollo Micro, Piccadily, Wockhardt, Bhagyanagar, Valiant) is what
he calls "Muft ka Laddu" — "significant remnants" left after the original capital was
withdrawn, "notionally free" yet "still very dear even if notionally free." The discipline is
mechanical: once a position has returned its cost, extract the principal and redeploy it,
leaving only the gain exposed. Distinct from a full "close the chapter on a high" exit
(`investor-psychology.md`) — here the position is deliberately kept, just de-risked to zero
cost basis. Caveat from the same post: he concedes he is "not sure when the promised
hockeystick will appear" on the Dynamatic remnant, i.e. a zero-cost position can still tie up
attention and hope past the point it deserves either.

## Optimise for avoiding drawdowns, not for catching upside; returns follow (phreakv6, rank #3, core-tier)

*Phreak's Thoughts, Ideas and Opinions*, post #330 (2026-09-01, love=83): "If you avoid
drawdowns, your returns will be spectacular. To avoid drawdowns, stay within what you
understand and never chase without having a sense of value." Stated as his core risk
philosophy: the two operational rules he ties drawdown-avoidance to are (1) only hold what you
actually understand (paired with his written negative-list of sectors he refuses to touch —
see `investor-psychology.md`) and (2) never buy without an anchored view of value, i.e. no
momentum-chasing into names where you can't say what they're worth. The framing inverts the
usual "how do I find the next multibagger" question — he treats compounding as the residual
that takes care of itself once the left tail is controlled, so the active work is on what to
avoid and when not to buy, not on maximising hit rate.

## Concentrate to build the portfolio, diversify to protect the gains — position sizing should change with portfolio size, not stay fixed (Vivek_6954, rank #5, elevated-tier)

*Vivek Gautam Portfolio*, post #1256 (2026-09-03): "Concentrated approach first to build up
the portfolio now diversified approach to preserve the gains as portfolio becomes large. 3 to
4% allocation in 30 to 35 names. Drawdown becomes tolerable. Depends on homework one ones own
temperament also." Reflecting on a ~14-year, ~100x journey (post #1245, love=26): the same
investor used concentration early (when a mistake is recoverable and upside matters more than
drawdown) and switched to a ~30-35 name, 3-4%-per-position book later (when preserving
already-compounded gains matters more than squeezing out more upside). He pairs this with
"ruthless churning" — converting underperforming/non-performing positions into new names, or
into better-performing existing ones, calling allocation quality "equally important as stock
selection" — and "pyramiding" (adding to positions on confirming triggers like good results)
as the mechanism for increasing allocation to winners rather than starting oversized.
